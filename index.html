A statewide zoning-reform impact model, piloted in Kauai County

Hawaii doesn't have a housing shortage because people won't build. It has one because almost nothing pencils for who actually lives here.

This tool measures what zoning reform could — and couldn't — fix. It starts with what Kauai's market looks like today, then runs real reform scenarios (more units per lot, density near transit, cheaper permits) through a development pro forma and an affordability check grounded in actual local incomes, not assumptions.

Baseline vs. policy intervention — the rule every reform below follows

Every scenario answers one question: what does a reform add on top of current law, not what a parcel's total theoretical capacity is. A parcel's current-code capacity (what it can already legally hold today) is computed first and subtracted off; only the capacity a reform adds beyond that baseline is ever reported as the reform's impact. This single rule is applied identically in all four reforms below — it's why each one's formula includes an existing_units_estimate or baseline_capacity subtraction term.

ADU (Accessory Dwelling Units)

What it does: models Act 39/SB3202's real, already-enacted mandate — at least 2 ADUs per residentially-zoned lot — against Kauai's actual current density code, to find which parcels can legally add a second unit today.

Assumptions: eligibility requires (1) a single-family-zoned (R-numbered) parcel, (2) an existing residential unit on it, and (3) the district's real units/acre allowance covering existing_units_estimate + 1 on that parcel's actual area. No owner-occupancy requirement is assumed (Kauai's code has none, confirmed directly against the ordinance text) and no parking constraint gates eligibility (see Parking Requirement Reduction below for why). existing_units_estimate assumes 1 unit per already-residential parcel — Kauai has no direct unit-count field, so this is a stated simplification, not a measured count.

Sources: Kauai County Code §8-4.2(b)/§8-4.6 (the real per-district units/acre density rule, confirmed against the ordinance text, not inferred from zoning labels); Act 39 (2024)/SB3202 (see the Existing Conditions legislation section above).

Formula:

eligible = zone_is_R_district AND has_existing_residential_unit
           AND (max_units_by_density >= existing_units_estimate + 1)
estimated_new_units_per_tract = count(eligible_parcels_in_tract) × absorption_rate

Impact on the numbers: 2,297 eligible parcels countywide → 144.7 raw estimated units at the 6.3% absorption-rate default → 108.2 feasibility-adjusted units (3 of 17 tracts fail feasibility entirely) → 0 affordability-constrained units (every Kauai household earns below the required income for the comps-implied price in every tract — see the Affordability-Constrained Demand Gate below).

Missing Middle (2–6 units)

What it does: models upzoning to the Challenge's own 2–6-unit benchmark ceiling, but only on parcels current density code restricts to exactly their existing unit count (the same single-family-restricted parcels the ADU scenario would otherwise also touch).

Assumptions: a parcel the district's density math already lets build past its current unit count (most larger R-6+ lots) is excluded — that's already-legal baseline capacity, not a reform effect, and counting it here would double-count it as "impact." AG/O/C-N/C-G parcels are excluded entirely (no units/acre figure exists to compute against). Not summed with the ADU scenario for the same parcel, since both draw on the same underlying density allowance — a combined number would need to take the max of the two, not their sum (not yet wired in).

Sources: same density ordinance as ADU (Kauai County Code §8-4.2(b)/§8-4.6); the 2–6-unit/3-story ceiling is the Challenge benchmark's own stated definition of "Missing Middle," not a Kauai-specific figure.

Formula:

eligible = baseline_capacity == 0   (currently single-family-restricted in practice)
additional_unit_capacity = max(0, 6 − existing_units_estimate)   [for eligible parcels only]
additional_estimated_units_per_tract = sum(additional_unit_capacity_in_tract) × absorption_rate

Impact on the numbers: 4,163.8 raw estimated units countywide at the absorption-rate default → 2,937.6 feasibility-adjusted units (29% gated out by the same per-tract feasible/infeasible pattern as ADU) → 0 affordability-constrained units, for the same reason as ADU: the for-sale product's comps-implied price clears no Kauai household's real income in any tract.

Transit-Oriented Density (TOD)

What it does: models the real, enacted floor-area-ratio (FAR) density standard from HB1409 (2025)/Act 159, HRS §206E-246(j), applied to parcels near Kauai Bus's real transit network — Kauai has no rail, so this models bus-corridor TOD, not a rail-station standard stretched to fit.

Assumptions: a "major transit stop" is a stop served by 2+ distinct weekday bus routes (58 of 126 weekday-served stops qualify — more destinations reachable, overlapping headways, the real distinction between a trunk/transfer stop and a single shuttle-loop stop). Eligibility requires a parcel to fall within both a ¾-mile radius of a major stop and a ¾-mile buffer of an HDOT-classified Principal or Minor Arterial corridor (Kuhio Hwy, Kaumualii Hwy) — stop-proximity alone flagged ~40% of the county and wasn't a usable zoning standard on its own. FAR defaults to the statute's base tier (4.0); a UI slider lets a user model the statute's higher 6.0/7.0 half-mile/quarter-mile tiers directly, since whether a bus corridor (vs. a rail station) can legally reach those tiers turns on an undefined statutory term ("locally preferred alternative"). Unit size (840 GSF) reuses the Lihu'e lowrise product's real OPSD-sourced figure as a stated stand-in — no Hawaii source for a TOD-specific unit size was found. Unlike ADU/Missing Middle, non-R zones (AG/O/C-N/C-G) are NOT excluded — the statute applies "for all uses permitted ... under underlying county zoning."

Sources: HB1409 (2025)/Act 159, HRS §206E-246(j) (the enacted FAR tiers); Kauai Bus GTFS feed (MobilityDatabase, NTD ID 90237) for stop/route data; HDOT HPMS Roads federal functional classification for the arterial-corridor definition; OPSD's Toward a Transit-Oriented Development Housing Investment Strategy (SCR162 report, Dec. 2023) for the ¾-mile proximity standard, the one real Hawaii standard found that's explicitly mode-agnostic (built for both rail and bus) rather than a rail standard applied to buses.

Formula:

tod_eligible = within_¾mi_of_major_stop AND within_¾mi_of_arterial_corridor
reform_capacity = floor(parcel_area_sqft × FAR / 840)      [FAR slider, default 4.0]
additional_capacity = max(0, reform_capacity − existing_units_estimate)   [tod_eligible parcels only]
additional_estimated_units_per_tract = sum(additional_capacity_in_tract) × absorption_rate

Impact on the numbers — an honest, unresolved finding: 13,004 of Kauai's ~25,000 parcels are TOD-eligible under this definition — 267,734 pre-feasibility estimated units at FAR 4.0, falling only to 190,779 units after the feasibility gate. This is implausible on its face (Kauai's entire 2023–2027 shortage is 4,914 units), and three different real, independently-sourced Hawaii TOD density mechanisms have now been tried here, all producing an implausible result applied blanket across this footprint. The dashboard shows this deliberately unresolved (an adjustable FAR slider with an on-page warning) rather than picking a fourth number and hoping it lands better — the real suspected problem is the eligibility footprint's breadth (over half the county), not the density figure itself. At the affordability gate, TOD's rental product fares better than ADU/MM's for-sale product (28–34% of households clear the rent bar in most tracts, since $3,708/month is a far lower bar than a $1M+ sale price) — 190,779 feasibility-adjusted units falls to 52,185 affordability-constrained units, a real ~73% cut on top of the magnitude problem above.

Parking Requirement Reduction

What it does to the housing numbers: nothing, directly. Parking does not gate ADU eligibility on Kauai — a parcel either qualifies for a second unit under the density rule above or it doesn't, and current code already requires only 1 off-street space per ADU either way. Removing that requirement doesn't make any additional parcel eligible, so this scenario reports the same eligible-parcel count as the ADU scenario (2,297), not a unit-count change. What actually changes is land freed on each eligible parcel that the parking requirement would otherwise consume.

Assumptions: 200 sqft/space (a standard surface-parking stall + maneuvering allowance) — a planning-literature figure, not Kauai-specific, the same evidentiary status as other inherited planning benchmarks in this model. This project deliberately does NOT apply a quantitative post-reform parking-supply reduction ratio to Kauai: Sohoni & Lee (2025) found parking reform's effect ranges from minimal (Grand Rapids, Hartford) to large (Champaign, Somerville) depending on a city's density and car-dependency — and Kauai's own real ACS figures (~5% non-car commute share, ~675 people/sq mi) sit below even the study's "minimal effect" anchor cities on both axes, meaning a numeric reduction ratio would be extrapolation past the regression's support, not interpolation within it.

Sources: Kauai County Code Chapter 8, Article 15/§8-1.4(i) (the real confirmed 1-space/ADU requirement); Sohoni & Lee, "Parking the Minimum Parking Requirement: Evidence from Nine U.S. Cities," Journal of Planning Education and Research 46(1), 2025 (doi.org/10.1177/0739456X251343047) — Table 3's real before/after parking-supply figures for 9 cities, used for the density/car-dependency comparison, not for a reduction ratio applied to Kauai; ACS 2022 5-year commute/density data for Kauai's own position on those same two axes.

Formula:

eligible_parcels = (same set as the ADU scenario -- unchanged by this reform)
parking_land_consumed_sqft_per_tract = count(eligible_parcels_in_tract) × 200

Impact on the numbers: 2,297 eligible parcels × 200 sqft/space = 459,400 sqft (10.5 acres) of parking land freed countywide if the requirement is eliminated — real, but modest at the scale of the county's 4,914-unit shortage, and zero additional units this model can attribute to the reform directly. This is why the Scenario Explorer labels this chart "Land freed, sqft (NOT units)" rather than a units column.

Development Fee Reduction & the feasibility gate

What it does: answers whether a parcel's zoning capacity is actually profitable to build, using a real Kauai-specific development pro forma — not just whether it's legal. This is the gate that converts every reform's "zoning capacity" into its "feasibility-adjusted" number above.

Assumptions: uses the "excess value" test (market value − total development cost − required developer profit ≥ 0) from a real Kaua'i-specific pro forma (Lihu'e Town Core submarket), not an invented cost structure. Land cost is allocated as $/building-GSF (the source table's land price per sqft of residential building area), not a true site-area/FAR-based allocation — a stated simplification, since no per-district FAR/land-value data exists for Kauai yet. Financing cost uses an average-outstanding-balance approximation (loan amount × rate × term × 0.5, plus origination fees), not a full monthly draw schedule. The reference sale price is scaled to each tract's own ACS median home value (a linear heuristic: a tract twice as expensive as the county median is assumed to sell new product at roughly twice the reference price) — cost inputs stay fixed at the Lihu'e table's figures everywhere, since no tract-level cost data exists.

Sources: State of Hawaii OPSD, "Financial Analysis of TOD Infrastructure Funding and Financing Options" (HR&A Advisors et al., 2023) — Real Estate Assumptions table, Lihu'e Town Core (Kaua'i) column, sourced in the original to Group Pacific/PBR Hawai'i (hard costs), HR&A (soft costs, developer profit), CBRE (financing terms), and CoStar/Zillow (sale prices); the Challenge's own mandated $50,000/unit standardized development fee; Honolulu DPP's Affordable Housing Requirement Incentives handout (Feb. 2025) for the real $37,000–$74,000 fee-component stack bounding the slider's range; ACS 2022 5-year median home value (B25077) for the tract-scaling denominator.

Formula:

hard_cost = unit_size_gsf × hard_cost_per_gsf        ($420/GSF townhome, 1,500 GSF → $630,000)
soft_cost = hard_cost × 0.15
land_cost = unit_size_gsf × $36/building-GSF
loan_amount = (hard_cost + soft_cost + land_cost) × 0.60   (60% loan-to-cost)
financing_cost = loan_amount × 7.0% × 2yrs × 0.5 + loan_amount × 2.5% (fees)
total_development_cost = hard + soft + land + financing + development_fee
sale_price = reference_price × (tract_median_home_value / countywide_median_home_value)
required_developer_profit = sale_price × 15%
excess_value = sale_price − total_development_cost − required_developer_profit
feasible = (excess_value ≥ 0)

Impact on the numbers: at the Challenge's $50,000 standardized fee, the Lihu'e reference townhome's excess_value is positive countywide, but 3 of 17 tracts (the below-median-home-value ones, where the scaled sale price can't clear this fixed cost structure) come back infeasible — the source of ADU's 108.2→ the same 108.2 figure (no tracts newly flip) and Missing Middle's 29% cut. A fee change (the fee-reduction slider) moves excess_value dollar-for-dollar: at the real Honolulu-sourced $37,000–$74,000 fee-component range, the maximum possible swing is too small to flip any of those 3 infeasible tracts to feasible — the "fee reduction alone won't close the gap" finding documented in the Scenario Explorer's own fee-reduction note.

Affordability-constrained demand gate

What it does: a demand-side question distinct from feasibility — of the comps-implied price a feasible project would charge, what share of a tract's own households could actually afford it? A tract can clear feasibility (a developer's return pencils at the going price) while still being a market of a handful of local households who could ever pay that price — this gate is how the dashboard catches that, rather than reporting feasibility alone as the finish line.

Assumptions: standard mortgage underwriting — 20% down, 30-year fixed, monthly principal+interest capped at 30% of gross income (the HUD/Census "cost-burdened" standard, not a project-invented threshold). Principal + interest ONLY — excludes property tax, homeowner's insurance, and HOA/CPR fees (common on Kauai condo/CPR units), since no Hawaii-specific property-tax-rate or insurance-cost source is in this project yet. This means required income is an UNDERSTATEMENT of the true bar — the real qualifying percentage is at or below what this model computes, never above it. Treats "% of tract households who could afford the unit" as a direct ceiling on units absorbed in that tract specifically — a conservative, locally-grounded assumption that undercounts regional demand (buyers from a wider commute-shed aren't counted), not a claim that every qualifying household is in the market this year (that's what the separate absorption rate is for — the two stack multiplicatively, not interchangeably).

Sources: Census ACS 5-year detailed table B19001 (Household Income in the Past 12 Months), tract-level, 16 real income brackets — not a tract median used as a stand-in; HiCentral (Honolulu Board of REALTORS) real mortgage rate quotes (hicentral.com/hawaii-mortgage-rates.php, confirmed 2026-10-15) — median of 8 Hawaii-market lenders' 30-yr fixed rate, 7.000%, excluding one non-Hawaii-market outlier; the countywide Zillow ZORI rent figure for TOD's rental product (no tract-level rent signal exists for Kauai).

Formula:

loan_amount = sale_price × (1 − 20%)
monthly_rate = 7.000% / 12;  n = 360 months
monthly_PI = loan_amount × [monthly_rate × (1+monthly_rate)^n] / [(1+monthly_rate)^n − 1]
required_income = (monthly_PI × 12) / 30%
pct_households_affording = share of the tract's real B19001 brackets with income ≥ required_income
                            (linear interpolation within whichever bracket the threshold falls inside)
affordability_adjusted_units = feasibility_adjusted_units × (pct_households_affording / 100)

Impact on the numbers — the headline finding: 0% of Kauai households clear the required-income bar in every single tract for the ADU/Missing Middle for-sale product — 108.2 feasibility-adjusted ADU units and 2,937.6 Missing Middle units both collapse to 0 affordability-constrained units countywide. The tracts where a project is feasible to build (high enough comps to cover cost + profit) are disproportionately Kauai's expensive tracts (Princeville/Hanalei North Shore) — $1.9M+ required sale prices, $400K+ required household income, which essentially no Kauai household has; the lower-priced tracts fail feasibility in the first place. There is essentially no overlap between "a developer could profitably build this" and "a Kauai household could afford to buy it," as currently modeled. The Existing Conditions "Household income & the affordability gap" chart is this same math run in the other direction, against the real sales comps directly.

Absorption rate — how much of a reform's capacity actually gets built

What it does: every reform's zoning capacity is scaled by this single rate before feasibility/affordability are even applied — it's the model's stand-in for "not every legally eligible parcel redevelops in a given decade."

Assumptions: Kauai's default is 6.3% (not a round guess) — derived from each area's average annual permit count as a share of its housing stock, scaled to a 10-year horizon. This is NOT the same quantity a prior build's 8% assumption approximated (no historical eligible-parcel baseline exists for Hawaii either); it's the best available empirical proxy, not a measured redevelopment rate for ADU/Missing-Middle-eligible parcels specifically. Exposed as a UI slider so this uncertainty is adjustable, not hidden behind a fixed number.

Sources: FRED Building Permits Survey (series BPPRIV015001/3/7/9, HIBPPRIV) × Hawaii DBEDT 2023 housing-unit estimates (CO-EST2023-HU-15) — full figures in data/reference/absorption_rate_benchmarks.csv.

Formula:

absorption_rate = (avg_annual_building_permits / existing_housing_stock) × 10 years

Geography: tract vs. place

Hawaii has no incorporated cities — the entire state outside county government is unincorporated — so "place" in this tool means Census Designated Place (CDP), the only real place-name geography TIGERweb publishes for Hawaii. Each tract is assigned to whichever CDP covers the largest share of its land area (a real GIS spatial join, not a centroid guess or a name lookup); tracts with under 10% overlap with any CDP are labeled "Rural Kaua'i (unincorporated)" rather than force-assigned to a place they barely touch. When rolling tracts up to places, counts and land area are summed; rates and medians (rent, price/sqft, density) are weighted by the relevant denominator (households, sales count, population) — never a naive average of per-tract numbers, which would overweight a 3-household tract as much as a 900-household one. The Leaflet map itself stays at tract resolution regardless of the toggle.

Known limitations

  • Kauai only, so far — Oahu, Maui, and Hawai'i County are scaffolded in the UI but have no real data yet.
  • The affordability gate's mortgage calculation is principal-and-interest only — it excludes property tax, insurance, and HOA/CPR fees, so the true required income is understated, not overstated.
  • Feasibility's revenue side scales comps-implied price one-sidedly; it is a stated simplification, not a full market-clearing model.
  • Zillow publishes no ZIP-level rent data for Kauai — only one countywide ZORI figure exists, so the rent chart necessarily mixes tract-level ACS medians with a single countywide line.
  • The "over time" sales trend is 17 real months (Feb 2025–Jun 2026) — real transactions, but still shorter than ZORI's own 2015+ history.
  • The TOD scenario's eligibility footprint (over half the county) produces an implausible unit count at every density mechanism tried so far — presented deliberately unresolved, not papered over.
  • The 6.3% absorption rate is a statewide permit-to-stock proxy, not a measured redevelopment rate for reform-eligible parcels specifically.
